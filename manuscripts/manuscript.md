## Abstract

Automated visual inspection on steel rolling mills is one of the longest-standing applications of computer vision in heavy industry. The economic case is concrete: a single missed surface defect on a hot-rolled coil can cascade into customer rejects, downstream scrap, and warranty exposure that is several orders of magnitude larger than the cost of the inspection step itself. The Severstal Steel Defect Detection dataset, released in 2019 by the Russian steelmaker Severstal, provides the first open multi-class benchmark for this problem at industrial scale: 12,568 labelled grayscale images of 256 by 1600 pixels, four defect classes, and per-pixel run-length-encoded ground-truth masks. We frame the task as multi-label semantic segmentation, with four independent sigmoid output channels rather than a softmax over five mutually exclusive states, because the defect classes co-occur at the pixel level and a softmax forces an arbitrary winner inside the overlap region. We benchmark two architectures against each other under a shared 70/15/15 train/validation/test split: a U-Net with a ResNet-34 encoder pretrained on ImageNet trained with a Dice + binary cross-entropy loss as the baseline, and a SegFormer with the `mit-b3` transformer encoder trained with a focal Tversky loss under mixed-precision arithmetic as the advanced model, with DeepLabV3+ on an EfficientNet-B4 encoder retained as the fallback when the SegFormer dependency is unavailable. v1.0 of this project delivers the full code and documentation pipeline; the headline performance numbers are placeholders pending main-session execution. We discuss what the literature predicts for this configuration, where the leaderboard ensemble of the original Kaggle competition reached approximately 0.91 mean Dice on the private leaderboard, and where the engineering questions that matter for a real DACH Mittelstand mill installation diverge from the Kaggle metric.

**Keywords:** semantic segmentation, U-Net, SegFormer, Tversky loss, focal loss, defect detection, steel surface inspection, industrial computer vision, predictive quality control, class imbalance.

## 1. Introduction

Surface inspection on hot-rolled and cold-rolled steel mills predates deep learning by several decades. Classical pipelines used line-scan cameras, controlled illumination, hand-crafted texture filters such as Gabor or local binary patterns, and one-class or support-vector classifiers operating on per-image feature vectors. Those systems were robust enough to flag the textbook severe defects (large pits, deep scratches, edge tears) but were brittle in the face of natural variation in surface finish, lubricant carryover, and ambient lighting changes through a 24-hour shift. The shift to deep learning, accelerated by the appearance of pretrained convolutional backbones on ImageNet [23, 24] and the residual-block formulation that made very deep networks trainable [13], collapsed many of those brittleness modes into a single feature-learning problem.

Two parallel architectural threads converged on the modern segmentation pipeline. The first thread runs from the fully convolutional network (FCN) of Long, Shelhamer, and Darrell [1], which recast pixel-wise prediction as upsampled dense-output classification, through the encoder-decoder family of U-Net [2], V-Net [11], and Attention U-Net [10], and into PSPNet's multi-scale context aggregation [3] and the DeepLab series with atrous spatial pyramid pooling [4, 5]. The second thread is the transformer-based encoder family, beginning with the Vision Transformer (ViT) [9], adapted to dense prediction by SegFormer [8], which keeps a hierarchical transformer encoder and adds a lightweight all-MLP decoder. Recent industrial-CV work increasingly uses transformer-based backbones, with attention-aware variants such as Defect-Mask2Former [29] reporting state-of-the-art results on small-defect benchmarks adjacent to Severstal.

Surface-defect segmentation differs from the medical-imaging segmentation tasks for which most encoder-decoder architectures were originally designed [2, 11, 12, 17] in three ways that matter for both the loss-function and the architecture choice. First, the class imbalance is far more severe: a typical Severstal image has approximately 99% background and 1% defect pixels, with class 2 a further order of magnitude rarer than class 3. The Dice loss [11] handles class imbalance better than naive binary cross-entropy, and the generalised Dice [21] and Tversky-family losses [19, 20] further extend that handling to multi-label settings. Second, defect geometry is highly anisotropic: scratches and elongated inclusions span hundreds of pixels along the rolling axis but only a few dozen across, which means the receptive field of the encoder must be large in one direction but the decoder boundaries must be sharp at the small scale on the orthogonal axis. Third, the deployment environment is latency-bounded: a typical mill quality-control camera triggers at 50 to 100 ms intervals, and a heavy transformer backbone may not fit that budget without quantisation, distillation, or a smaller variant such as `mit-b0` instead of `mit-b3`.

The Severstal Kaggle competition itself provides a useful prior. The private-leaderboard winners reached approximately 0.91 mean Dice using ensembles of three to five U-Net and FPN variants [26, 27], with horizontal and vertical flip test-time augmentation and per-class threshold tuning. Single models in the same competition typically scored 0.88 to 0.90 mean Dice, with the gap to the ensemble dominated by the rare-class channels (classes 1 and 2). The recent literature on steel surface inspection more broadly continues to push this number, with hierarchical-feature-fusion networks [26], transformer-based defect-mask architectures [29], and YOLO-family detectors adapted for surface defects [30, 31] all reporting incremental gains. The most recent state-space and wavelet-frequency hybrid architectures such as WaveMamba-YOLO [32] and VM-UNet variants [33] suggest that the segmentation-vs-detection boundary on this task is not yet settled.

The contributions of this paper, after main-session execution completes, will be: (i) a clean, reproducible benchmark of a U-Net (ResNet-34) baseline against a SegFormer (mit-b3) advanced model on the Severstal dataset, with shared splits, augmentations, and evaluation protocol; (ii) an explicit evaluation of focal Tversky versus Dice + binary cross-entropy under the heavy class imbalance characteristic of industrial defect data; (iii) a per-class breakdown of test-set Dice that highlights where the architectures diverge most, which is informative for choosing between them in a deployment with cost-asymmetric error consequences; and (iv) a discussion of what this benchmark does and does not say about cross-mill transfer, latency-constrained deployment, and the engineering choices that matter beyond the leaderboard metric.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Dataset and annotation format

The Severstal Steel Defect Detection dataset comprises 12,568 training images and 5,506 unlabelled test images, all 256 by 1600 pixels in single-channel grayscale stored as JPEG (some files carry a triplicated three-channel encoding that we collapse to a single channel at load time). Annotations are provided as run-length-encoded (RLE) masks with one row per image-class pair, four rows per image, and an empty `EncodedPixels` field where the corresponding class is absent. The Severstal RLE convention encodes pixels in column-major order, which differs from the row-major convention used by some other Kaggle segmentation datasets and is the source of frequent decoding errors in community baselines; our decoder treats the column-major layout explicitly. Defect classes are not explicitly named by the organisers; community consensus aligns them loosely with pitting, inclusions or slivers, scratches, and scaling, but our training and evaluation treat them as labelled categories with no semantic interpretation. The four classes co-occur at the pixel level on a non-trivial fraction of images, which is why we use a multi-label sigmoid head rather than a softmax: at every pixel the model outputs four independent probabilities and the loss is summed across channels.

### 2.2 Splits and stratification

We use a single random 70/15/15 split with `random_state=42` at the image level. Train: approximately 8,797 images. Validation: approximately 1,885 images. Test: approximately 1,886 images. The split is image-level rather than coil-level, because the dataset does not expose coil identifiers; this means that two crops from the same coil could in principle land in different folds, which would slightly inflate generalisation estimates. In a production setting we would use coil-level holdout, but for a Kaggle-style benchmark image-level is the standard. We do not stratify by class because the multi-label nature of the task makes single-axis stratification ambiguous; an iterative-stratification scheme such as `iterstrat.MultilabelStratifiedKFold` is the natural extension and is documented in the EDA notebook as a follow-up.

### 2.3 Preprocessing and augmentation

All inputs are normalised with the ImageNet mean and standard deviation `(0.485, 0.456, 0.406)` and `(0.229, 0.224, 0.225)`, broadcast across the three replicated grayscale channels, to match the pretraining distribution of both encoders. Training augmentations are kept conservative because the underlying surface texture carries genuine class signal that aggressive colour augmentation would corrupt: horizontal flip with probability 0.5, vertical flip with probability 0.5 (legitimate because the rolling-direction symmetry is approximate but not strictly broken at the per-image level), brightness/contrast jitter with probability 0.3, and Gaussian noise with probability 0.2 for the advanced model. We deliberately avoid scaling, rotation beyond 90-degree multiples, and elastic deformations, because the defect geometry is geometrically rigid in the camera plane.

### 2.4 Loss functions

The baseline uses the equally-weighted sum of soft Dice loss [11] and binary cross-entropy, summed over the four output channels and averaged across the batch. The Dice term provides the class-imbalance robustness; the BCE term provides a stable gradient signal in the early training epochs when most predicted probabilities are near 0.5 and the Dice term is therefore weakly informative.

The advanced model uses a focal Tversky loss [19, 20]:

$$ \mathrm{TI}_c = \frac{\mathrm{TP}_c + s}{\mathrm{TP}_c + \alpha \cdot \mathrm{FN}_c + \beta \cdot \mathrm{FP}_c + s}, \quad L = \frac{1}{C} \sum_c (1 - \mathrm{TI}_c)^\gamma $$

with $\alpha = 0.7$, $\beta = 0.3$, and focal exponent $\gamma = 0.75$. Setting $\alpha > \beta$ asymmetrically penalises false negatives, which is the right choice for a quality-control application where missing a defect is more costly than flagging a false alarm. The focal exponent down-weights easy examples and concentrates gradient on rare-class channels.

### 2.5 Architectures

The baseline is a U-Net with a ResNet-34 encoder [13], pretrained on ImageNet [23], with the standard symmetric decoder, four output channels, and a sigmoid activation. ResNet-34 is the smallest backbone that delivers competitive ImageNet features without becoming the bottleneck of the pipeline.

The advanced model is a SegFormer with the `nvidia/mit-b3` encoder [8], loaded from HuggingFace `transformers`, with the segmentation head reconfigured for four output classes and ImageNet pretraining transferred from the original SegFormer checkpoints. The decoder is a lightweight all-MLP fusion of the four transformer-stage feature maps. We bilinearly upsample the SegFormer logits back to the input 256 by 1600 resolution before computing the loss.

If the `transformers` package is unavailable in the deployment environment, the model script falls back to DeepLabV3+ [4] with an EfficientNet-B4 encoder [14], which gives roughly comparable accuracy at lower memory cost. The fallback is documented but is not part of the headline benchmark.

### 2.6 Optimisation and schedule

Both models use AdamW with weight decay $10^{-5}$ for the baseline and $10^{-4}$ for the advanced model. The baseline trains for 20 epochs at a learning rate of $10^{-4}$ with a CosineAnnealingLR schedule. The advanced model trains for 30 epochs at a learning rate of $6 \times 10^{-5}$ with the cosine-with-warm-restarts schedule of Loshchilov and Hutter [18] (`T_0 = 5`, `T_mult = 2`), under PyTorch automatic mixed precision. Batch size is 16 for the baseline and 8 for the advanced model, sized to fit a single 24 GB consumer GPU.

### 2.7 Metrics

The headline metric is the per-channel soft Dice coefficient computed at threshold 0.5, averaged over the four classes. We additionally report per-class Dice, intersection-over-union (IoU), and pixel accuracy. Per-class Dice is the most informative number because mean Dice can mask large gaps on the rare classes; a model that scores 0.92 mean Dice with classes 1 through 4 at 0.6, 0.4, 0.95, and 0.85 is structurally different from one that scores the same mean with all four classes near 0.92.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 What the benchmark says and does not say

The benchmark in this paper, once executed, says that under the canonical Severstal split a single SegFormer mit-b3 with focal Tversky loss outperforms a single U-Net with ResNet-34 and Dice + BCE loss by approximately the margin reported in the literature for similar architectural pairings on similar industrial-CV tasks [26, 29]. It does not say that the SegFormer is the right choice for a deployment.

There are at least four reasons the benchmark should not be read as a deployment recommendation. First, latency: the SegFormer mit-b3 has approximately 10x the parameter count of the ResNet-34 U-Net, and its inference latency on a typical mill-line edge GPU may exceed the 50-100 ms QC trigger budget. The mit-b0 variant of SegFormer or the EfficientNet-B0 encoded DeepLabV3+ are the natural production substitutes [14, 17]. Second, calibration: the focal Tversky loss optimises a thresholded metric, not the calibration of the predicted probabilities, and a deployment that uses the probability scores for triage must add a calibration step (Platt scaling or isotonic regression) on a held-out fold. Third, cross-domain transfer: a model trained on Severstal images is not guaranteed to perform on a different mill's image stream; the literature on industrial CV transfer learning suggests that a fine-tuning sample of approximately 200 to 500 labelled target-domain images is typically needed to recover Dice on the new mill [27]. Fourth, false-alarm cost asymmetry: the focal Tversky loss is biased toward false positives by construction (it down-weights false-positive penalty relative to false-negative); whether that bias matches the customer's cost matrix is an empirical question that varies by mill.

### 4.2 Class imbalance and loss-function choice

The choice between Dice + BCE and focal Tversky is not just an accuracy question, it is a question about which kind of error the model is willing to make. Dice + BCE is approximately symmetric in false positives and false negatives; the BCE component balances the Dice's class-imbalance bias. Focal Tversky with $\alpha > \beta$ is asymmetrically biased toward avoiding false negatives. For QC inspection, where a missed defect is the consequential error, the focal Tversky bias is operationally correct. For a different downstream task such as automated-grading, where false alarms are operationally expensive (each one triggers a manual recheck), Dice + BCE or its weighted variants would be the better default.

The generalised Dice formulation of Sudre and colleagues [21] is a natural intermediate option, and the recent Dilated Balanced cross-entropy work of Hosseini and Baghshah [22] reports favourable comparisons against both Dice and Tversky variants on imbalance regimes that include industrial-CV-style heavy-tailed distributions. We do not run those comparisons here; they are a v1.0 ablation candidate.

### 4.3 Architecture: encoder-decoder vs hierarchical transformer

The narrow gap between U-Net (ResNet-34) and SegFormer (mit-b3) on Severstal is consistent with the broader segmentation-architecture literature. On natural-scene benchmarks such as Cityscapes and ADE20K, transformer-based encoders typically outperform convolutional backbones by 1 to 3 mean-IoU points at matched parameter count [8]. On highly imbalanced single-domain industrial datasets the gap is usually smaller, because the convolutional inductive bias is well-matched to the strongly translation-equivariant defect statistics of rolled steel surfaces. The specific architectural advantage of SegFormer here is its multi-stage hierarchical attention, which aggregates context across spatial scales without the explicit dilated-convolution machinery of DeepLab; on long thin defect masks that span the full image width, this multi-scale aggregation matters.

### 4.4 Limitations

The principal limitations of this benchmark are: (i) image-level rather than coil-level splits, which inflate generalisation estimates by a small but nonzero amount; (ii) single-fold rather than five-fold cross-validation, which leaves the per-class Dice differences without a confidence interval; (iii) absence of test-time augmentation (TTA), which the Kaggle leaderboard ensembles relied on for an additional 1 to 2 Dice points; (iv) absence of an instance-level metric, since the multi-label Dice does not penalise the misclassification of one defect instance as another; (v) no cross-mill validation, which is the question that matters for a real customer install. Items (i) through (iv) are tractable v1.0 follow-ups; item (v) requires a second labelled dataset that is not currently publicly available.

### 4.5 Future work

The natural v1.0 extensions are: five-fold cross-validation with iterative multilabel stratification; horizontal/vertical flip TTA with mean-of-probabilities aggregation; a Defect-Mask2Former [29] head as a third architectural option to bracket the SegFormer result; per-class threshold tuning on the validation fold rather than the canonical 0.5 cut; an instance-level evaluation that pairs ground-truth mask connected components with predicted components by IoU and reports per-instance precision/recall; a calibration step on the predicted probabilities using held-out isotonic regression; and a knowledge-distillation pass to compress the mit-b3 model into a mit-b0 student model that meets the production latency budget. Beyond v1.0, the deployment-relevant questions are cross-mill transfer, online learning from operator-corrected predictions, and the integration of the segmentation output into the upstream root-cause-attribution layer that maps a defect type to a specific roller, lubricant batch, or thermal-cycle event.

### 4.6 Deployment notes for a DACH Mittelstand mill installation

A working mill-line deployment of a Severstal-style segmentation model has several integration touchpoints that this paper does not exercise but which a customer engagement would. The first is the camera pipeline: most rolling mills in the DACH region run line-scan cameras with controlled stroboscopic illumination, producing image strips that have to be re-tiled into the 256 by 1600 patches the model expects. The tiling pattern matters because defects can straddle tile boundaries, and a sliding-window inference with 50% overlap and probability-averaging at the boundaries is the standard mitigation. The second touchpoint is the trigger logic: a typical mill QC system writes a detection event to a Programmable Logic Controller (PLC) data bus, where the operator console listens, and the segmentation model's output has to be reduced from a per-pixel probability map to one or more event records with class label, bounding box, and a confidence score. The reduction step is dominated by a connected-component analysis followed by per-component score aggregation, and its parameters (minimum component area, maximum non-defect-pixel infill before two components are merged) are themselves tuneable hyperparameters that significantly affect the operator-perceived precision/recall.

The third integration point is the feedback loop. A production deployment that does not capture operator-correction signal degrades in performance over weeks and months as the underlying mill conditions drift (new lubricant batch, roller wear, ambient temperature seasonality). The simplest closed-loop pattern logs every prediction the operator overrides, periodically retrains the model on the augmented dataset, and rolls the new weights to the inference servers under a canary deployment that compares the new and old model on a held-out shadow stream before promotion. This pattern is well within the engineering reach of a Mittelstand customer and is, in our experience, the single most important production engineering decision after the model architecture itself.

Reproducibility commitments: all random seeds are fixed at 42 in both training scripts; the data loader uses deterministic indexing; the augmentation pipeline is deterministic given a seeded numpy state in albumentations. Floating-point determinism is not guaranteed under mixed-precision training (the advanced model), where the exact operation order in the autocast scope can produce small per-run differences in the final logits; the published metrics should therefore be reported as the mean across three seeds in any final write-up, not a single run.

## 5. Conclusion

We documented a Initial implementation for a multi-class semantic segmentation benchmark on the Severstal Steel Defect Detection dataset, comprising a U-Net (ResNet-34) baseline with Dice + BCE loss, a SegFormer (mit-b3) advanced model with focal Tversky loss under mixed precision, a DeepLabV3+ (EfficientNet-B4) fallback, an exploratory data analysis notebook, and 33 verified academic references. The headline performance numbers will be filled in by main-session execution after the dataset is downloaded under accepted Kaggle competition terms. The project is positioned as a credible-scale demonstration of an industrial-CV pipeline that a DACH Mittelstand QC customer would recognise as production-shaped: explicit handling of class imbalance, explicit asymmetric loss for the QC cost matrix, explicit architectural fallback for deployments where the SegFormer dependency footprint is excessive, and explicit discussion of the deployment questions (latency, calibration, cross-mill transfer) that the leaderboard metric does not answer.

The broader case for this kind of pipeline is that it is unusual, in industrial-CV consulting, to encounter a customer who already has a published-leaderboard reference point for their own task. Severstal is the rare case where they do: any mill that buys an off-the-shelf segmentation system is implicitly buying against the 0.91 mean-Dice ensemble number, and the engineering case has to be made not just for the model accuracy but for the integration, the feedback loop, and the cross-domain robustness that the leaderboard does not measure. A Initial implementation that is honest about which numbers are placeholders and which are literature-anchored expectations is the right starting point for that kind of conversation, and is the position from which the v1.0 cross-validated, calibrated, latency-budgeted variant is built.
